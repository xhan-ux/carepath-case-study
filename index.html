01 · The problem

People shouldn't need to understand the system before the system helps them.

Public-service journeys often span unfamiliar terminology, different offices, forms, queues and uncertain handoffs. The interface problem is not just finding information — it is knowing what to do next.

“The real problem isn't always finding a service. It's knowing what to do next.”
  • People can arrive at the wrong place or without the right information.
  • Confirmation screens often tell you what you booked, not what you should do now.
  • When a queue or room changes, the user has to ask someone to verify the new state.
  • The official service may be correct but still difficult to navigate without context.

02 · Process

Build the journey before polishing the interface.

I treated the prototype as a service loop rather than a collection of screens: intent → explanation → official handoff → visit → live updates → completion.

Day 01

Frame the service problem

Mapped the public-service journey around moments where people need an answer, a decision or a next action.

Day 02

Define the shared journey model

Established one state model that could drive patient and staff experiences instead of designing isolated flows.

Day 03

Prototype the healthcare route

Started with the deepest journey: appointment, arrival, registration, queue, consultation and completion.

Day 04–06

Wire the staff side

Added registration, doctor, lab and pharmacy workstations so patient updates had a real source of truth.

Day 07–08

Add accessibility and uncertainty states

Built readable hierarchy, language/read-aloud controls, room-change messaging and clear current/next states.

Day 09–10

Test the whole loop

Ran all four journeys end to end, switched languages both directions, checked back/exit/restart, verified official links.

Walkthrough

Healthcare, the deepest journey

Healthcare is the fully working part of the prototype — booking guidance, a live visit tracker, and the staff side that actually drives it. Four moments from that journey:

01 — Starting from intent

What do you need today?

No department names. Five plain-language entry points, and a standing reminder that CarePath isn't the hospital — it's the route to it.

The same pattern one level up: one journey, any service — healthcare, pension, certificates and grievances all sit behind the same “start with what you need” front door.

02 — Booking, without teaching ORS first

Two questions, then a real handoff

Someone without an appointment doesn't need an account or a login to get oriented — just two questions before CarePath explains the government booking flow in plain language and hands off to the real service.

The follow-through matters as much as the explanation: a numbered guide to the official Online Registration System, with an explicit “you are leaving CarePath” notice before the handoff — never a fake booking form pretending to be the real thing.

03 — One next step, live, for the whole visit

From confirmation to “go to Room 204”

This is the core loop: a single journey view that updates in place as the visit progresses, instead of a static confirmation the user has to reinterpret each time.

Actual prototype states — Four states of one screen, not four screens: appointment confirmed → checked in and waiting → a room change surfaced as a toast the moment it happens → called. The “What changed” log underneath every state exists so a person can trust the current instruction without needing to ask a human to confirm it.
For a visit that's already booked, one lookup — mobile number plus appointment ID — opens straight into the current step, no account needed.

04 — The other half of the loop

Staff side: the same journey, from the desk

The patient's “your turn” isn't magic — it's a staff member at Registration, Doctor, Lab or Pharmacy pressing a real action. Building this side first is what made the patient side honest.

One shared state, four workstations. Marking a patient arrived on this screen is the same event that moves their journey view forward on the patient's phone.

Design system snapshot

Quiet, trustworthy, plain-spoken

Deep teal for anything trustworthy or actionable, soft mint for information at rest, warm off-white underneath everything. Amber is reserved for one job — telling you something just changed.

Honesty check

What's really working vs. what's simulated

FUNCTIONAL IN THIS PROTOTYPE
  • Every screen shown here is a real, navigable state — no dummy buttons or dead-end cards.
  • Journey step, queue position and room all update from one shared state, live.
  • Staff actions and the patient's view are wired to the same data.
  • Accessibility, read-aloud and language controls are present on every screen.
STILL SYNTHETIC / OUT OF SCOPE
  • ORS is a real external app; CarePath explains it but doesn't submit bookings there.
  • Patients, tokens and hospital data are synthetic demo records.
  • No SMS/USSD/IVR infrastructure — the keypad flow is a concept, not a live channel.
  • Translations are directional and need native-speaker review before real use.

Reflection

Not “a nicer government form”

The decision that shaped everything else was to stop competing with the government system and start reducing the uncertainty around it — before, during and after the transaction.

That moves the real question from “does this look better than the official portal” to “can someone understand what they need, prepare correctly, reach the right official service, and know what happens next.” The keypad concept pushes that further: a family member or a registration clerk can carry someone through the journey who'd never open the app themselves.
