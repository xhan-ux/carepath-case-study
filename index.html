THE PROBLEM

People shouldn't need to understand the system before the system helps them.

Public services can involve unfamiliar terminology, multiple steps, different offices, forms, queues, and uncertainty about what happens next. The challenge was to turn that complexity into a journey people can actually follow.

“The real problem isn't always finding a service. It's knowing what to do next.”

DESIGNING THE SYSTEM

Not another government portal.
A layer that makes the journey legible.

CarePath is designed around the user's journey rather than the organisation's internal structure.

01Journey over departments

Organise information around what the person is trying to accomplish.

02Progress over ambiguity

Always make the current step and next action visible.

03Access over assumptions

Consider different devices, connectivity, language, and assisted access.

ACCESSIBILITY

Good service design has to work for the person who has the fewest options.

01

Clear typography and readable information hierarchy.

02

Short, unobtrusive interactions instead of interface noise.

03

Consideration for lower-connectivity and assisted-access contexts.

04

Language and accessibility controls are part of the experience, not an afterthought.

06

REFLECTION

The interface is only one part of the solution.

The strongest outcome of CarePath was reframing the problem from “How do we build another service portal?” to “How do we make a complicated service journey understandable?”

That shift changed the structure of the product: progress became a first-class element, the next step became explicit, and accessibility became part of the core journey rather than a finishing layer.

Designing for clarity means designing for the moment when someone doesn't know what to do next.
